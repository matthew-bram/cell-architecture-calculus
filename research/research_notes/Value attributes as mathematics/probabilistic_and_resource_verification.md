## Key question 1: Probabilistic model checking (PCTL, CSL, PRISM, Storm), reward structures, and composition of reliability/availability from components

### Takeaway
Probabilistic model checkers state reliability as a bounded-reachability probability (`P>=0.99 [ F<=1 "served" ]`), availability as a steady-state probability (`S>=0.999 [ "up" ]`), and cost/latency as expected accumulated reward (`R{"time"}=? [ F "done" ]`) over a DTMC/CTMC/MDP; composition of components is by parallel composition of modules whose product state space is then solved numerically, not by a closed-form algebra, and there is at least one published application of exactly this to microservice Retry and Circuit Breaker patterns.

### Cited Findings

**Logics and property syntax (PRISM)**
- PRISM supports discrete-time models (DTMCs, PTAs), continuous-time models (CTMCs) and nondeterministic models (MDPs); "PCTL is used for specifying properties of discrete-time models such as DTMCs or PTAs ... CSL is an extension of PCTL for CTMCs." [sum] — [PRISM manual, Property Specification](https://www.prismmodelchecker.org/manual/PropertySpecification/Introduction)
- P-operator examples from the manual: `P>=1 [ F "terminate" ]`, `P<0.1 [ F<=100 num_errors > 5 ]`, `P=? [ !proc2_terminate U proc1_terminate ]`, `Pmax=? [ F<=T messages_lost > 10 ]` (the last for MDPs). Time-bounded reachability `F<=100` is the CSL bounded-until form. [sum] — [PRISM manual, Property Specification](https://www.prismmodelchecker.org/manual/PropertySpecification/Introduction)
- S-operator (steady state, i.e. long-run availability-style properties): `S<0.01 [ num_sensors < min_sensors ]`, `S=? [ queue_size / max_size > 0.75 ]`; the manual describes it as evaluating "the long-run probability". [sum] — [PRISM manual, Property Specification](https://www.prismmodelchecker.org/manual/PropertySpecification/Introduction)
- R-operator forms: Boolean `R bound [ rewardprop ]` with bound in `<r, <=r, >r, >=r`; numerical `R=?`, `Rmin=?`, `Rmax=?`. Reward-property kinds: reachability `F prop`, co-safe LTL `F (prop1 & (F prop2))`, cumulative `C<=t`, total `C`, instantaneous `I=t`, steady-state `S`. Examples: `R<=9.5 [ F z=2 ]`, `R=? [ C<=15.5 ]`, `R<4.4 [ I=100 ]`, `R{"num_failures"}=? [ C<=10.0 ]`, `R{2}=? [ F step=final ]`; discounting `Rmin=? [ F{discount=0.9} "goal" ]`. [sum] — [PRISM manual, Reward-based Properties](https://www.prismmodelchecker.org/manual/PropertySpecification/Reward-basedProperties)

**Logics and property syntax (Storm)**
- Storm's property language: `P{op}t [ phi ]`, `P=? [ phi ]`, `Pmin=?`/`Pmax=?` for nondeterministic models; `R{op}t [ r ]`, `R=?`, named rewards `R{"name"}{op}t [ r ]`; a `T` (time) operator `T<=t [ r ]`, `T=?` for continuous-time models; path formulae `a U b`, `F b`, bounded until `a U{op}k b`, `G a`; long-run average `LRA{op}t [a]` and `R=? [ LRA ]`; conditional `P=? [ F a || F b ]`; multi-objective `multi(P>=0.3 [ F "one" ], P>=0.5 [ G "two" ])`. Example `Pmax=? [ Pmin>0.2 [ F<=10 "elected" ] U x+y=10 ]`. Model types named: DTMC, CTMC, MDP, Markov automata. [sum] — [Storm documentation, Properties](https://www.stormchecker.org/documentation/background/properties.html)

**Reliability/availability/cost via rewards on CTMCs, applied to microservices**
- Mendonça, Aderaldo, Cámara, Garlan, "Model-Based Analysis of Microservice Resiliency Patterns" (ICSA 2020): "we use the PRISM probabilistic model checker to analyze the behavior of the Retry and Circuit Breaker resiliency patterns as continuous-time Markov chains (CTMC) ... to quantify the impact of applying each resiliency pattern on multiple quality attributes, as well as to determine how to best tune their parameters to deal with varying service availability conditions". They claim "this is the first approach that applies probabilistic model checking to analyze the behavior of popular microservice patterns." [src] — [ICSA 2020 PDF](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~jcm567/files/ICSA2020.pdf)
- Reward encoding of time in PRISM in that paper: `rewards "total_time" true : 1; endrewards` "instructs PRISM to accrue reward at rate 1 in every state of the model, enabling quantification of total execution time." The property class `R^r=? [F φ]` "allows quantifying the reward r accrued along system paths leading to states that eventually satisfy the state formula φ", e.g. `R{"contention_time"}=? [F end]` with a state reward "(under_contention, 1)". [src] — [ICSA 2020 PDF](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~jcm567/files/ICSA2020.pdf)
- Composition in that paper is by PRISM module parallel composition with shared action labels: client service, proxy, target service, and a Retry module or Circuit Breaker module that "can play the role of the client service from the perspective of the proxy, and the role of the proxy from the perspective of the client service"; shared actions are renamed (`r_req`, `r_res`, `r_fail`; `cb_req`, `cb_res`, `cb_fail`) to insert the pattern between client and proxy. [src] — [ICSA 2020 PDF](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~jcm567/files/ICSA2020.pdf)
- Availability of the target service is a model parameter AV in [0,1] converted to a recovery rate: "MRR = AV / (MTBF * (1 − AV)) for 0 ≤ AV < 1; IR for AV = 1, where MTBF is the target service's mean time between failures and IR is the instantaneous rate." Quality attributes analysed: Expected Total Time and Expected Contention Time. Finding: "properly configured, both patterns can significantly reduce resource contention at the client-side compared to a naive approach of continuously retrying failed requests, with only a moderate incr[ease in total time]"; exponential backoff "directly affects the client service's expected total execution time when the target service has low availability". [src] — [ICSA 2020 PDF](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~jcm567/files/ICSA2020.pdf)
- Search-summary claim (from a PRISM lecture/case-study set): the R operator with a "time" reward structure "can be used to calculate the mean-time-to-failure of the system, i.e., the expected amount of time that elapses before the first failure occurs." [sum] — [PRISM CTMC case studies slides](https://www.prismmodelchecker.org/lectures/biss07/06-ctmc%20case%20studies.pdf); see also [PRISM: Probabilistic Model Checking for Performance and Reliability Analysis](https://www.prismmodelchecker.org/papers/acmper_prismperf.pdf) (title only; not read).
- Other distributed-systems applications surfaced by search (titles only, not read): "Scalable Reliability Modelling of RAID Storage Subsystems" [arXiv 1508.02055](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.02055); "PCRAFT: Capacity Planning for Dependable Stateless Services" [arXiv 2206.07368](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07368); "Transient Reward Approximation for Continuous-Time Markov Chains" [arXiv 1212.1251](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1212.1251). [sum]

**Mechanized probabilistic model checking (Isabelle)**
- The Isabelle AFP entry "Markov Models" (Hölzl and Nipkow, 2012-01-03): "This is a formalization of Markov models in Isabelle/HOL. It builds on Isabelle's probability theory. The available models are currently Discrete-Time Markov Chains and a extensions of them with rewards." The entry page lists DTMC, CTMC, MDP, probabilistic model checking of pCTL formulas, rewards, and case studies (ZeroConf IPv4 allocation, Crowds anonymity, gossip broadcast); it depends on HOL-Probability, the Coinductive library and Gauss-Jordan elimination. [src for the abstract, sum for the list] — [AFP Markov_Models](https://isa-afp.org/entries/Markov_Models.html)
- Hölzl and Nipkow, "Verifying pCTL model checking": proves soundness and completeness of the pCTL model-checking algorithm in Isabelle/HOL, "defining Markov chains by transition matrices and formalizing the corresponding probability measure on sets of paths, with formalization of pCTL formulas including unbounded cumulated rewards." [sum] — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262238461_Verifying_pCTL_model_checking); Hölzl, "Markov chains and Markov decision processes in Isabelle/HOL" (JAR 2017) [PDF](https://www.cs.vu.nl/~jhl890/pub/hoelzl2017mdp.pdf) (not read); "Interactive verification of Markov chains: Two distributed protocol case studies" [arXiv 1212.3870](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1212.3870) (not read). [sum]

### Inferences
- The three value attributes map onto the three operators: reliability (probability a run completes) is a `P` reachability property; availability (fraction of time up) is an `S` steady-state property on a CTMC with up/down states, or equivalently `R=? [ LRA ]` of an indicator reward in Storm; performance and cost are `R` properties with a rate reward (time) or an impulse reward (per-operation cost), and latency SLOs are time-bounded reachability `P>=0.99 [ F<=1 "served" ]`.
- "Composition" in this tradition means synchronous product of component chains (PRISM modules with shared actions, as in the ICSA 2020 paper); the quantitative answer is computed on the product, so there is no general closed-form compositional rule beyond special structures (independent series/parallel, see Key question 2). A cell-and-event model is structurally a product of modules; each cell's reaction function is a transition relation, and attaching rates or probabilities to events is what turns it into a DTMC/CTMC.
- The Isabelle work is the closest existing mechanization of "model checking a Markov chain inside a proof assistant"; no Lean 4 equivalent of Markov_Models (DTMC + pCTL + rewards as a library) was found (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- The PRISM manual fetches returned summaries rather than page text; the operator syntax is consistent across the PRISM and Storm summaries and with memory, but exact wording of the manual was not read.
- Numerical results from the ICSA 2020 paper (figures 9–11) are in plots, not extracted text; only the qualitative conclusions were read.
- No fetched source gives a textbook statement of the CTMC steady-state availability computation (solving πQ = 0) or the transient-reliability computation (uniformization); these are standard [mem] but uncited here.
- No Lean 4 formalization of PCTL/CSL or DTMC/CTMC model checking was found by search.

## Key question 2: SLO mathematics as practiced (error budgets, burn rates, series/parallel availability, tail latency amplification)

### Takeaway
Industry SLO arithmetic is finite, rational arithmetic: an error budget is a count (or ratio) of allowed bad events over a window, burn rate is the ratio of observed error rate to the budgeted rate, the budget consumed by an alert is burn rate × window / period, series availability multiplies, parallel unavailability multiplies, and fan-out tail latency follows 1 − (1 − p)^n, which Dean and Barroso quantify as 63% of requests slow when 100 servers each have a 1-in-100 chance of being slow.

### Cited Findings

**Error budgets and burn rate (Google SRE Workbook, "Alerting on SLOs")**
- Error budget: "the number of allowed bad events within a defined SLO period." Burn rate: "how fast, relative to the SLO, the service consumes the error budget." With a 99.9% SLO over 30 days, a constant 0.1% error rate is burn rate 1. [sum] — [SRE Workbook, Alerting on SLOs](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)
- Table (99.9% SLO, 30-day window): burn rate 1 → 0.1% errors → budget exhausted in 30 days; 2 → 0.2% → 15 days; 10 → 1% → 3 days; 1,000 → 100% → 43 minutes. [sum] — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)
- Budget consumed by an alert window: "burn rate × alerting window size / period". [sum] — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)
- Recommended multiwindow, multi-burn-rate configuration for a 99.9% SLO: Page at burn rate 14.4 over 1 h long window / 5 min short window (2% of budget); Page at 6 over 6 h / 30 min (5%); Ticket at 1 over 3 days / 6 h (10%). The alert fires when both the long and the short window exceed the threshold. [sum] — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)
- Threshold expressed as an error ratio: `job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate{window} > (burn_rate * SLO_error_ratio)`, e.g. `job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate1h > (14.4 * 0.001)`. [sum] — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)

**Series and parallel availability (classic MTBF/MTTR)**
- Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). In series, failure rates add: λ_system = λ_1 + λ_2 + …, and system MTBF is the reciprocal of the sum. Parallel availability of two units: 1 − (1 − A)(1 − A), "subtracting the probability of both stopping simultaneously from 100%". For two identical parallel units, MTBF_parallel ≈ MTBF² / (2 × MTTR). [sum] — [BMC, System Reliability & Availability Calculations](https://blogs.bmc.com/system-reliability-availability-calculations/?print-posts=pdf); [QNX High Availability docs](https://www.qnx.com/developers/docs/6.5.0SP1.update/com.qnx.doc.neutrino_cookbook/s1_ha.html); [reliamag MTBF/MTTR guide](https://reliamag.com/guides/how-to-calculate-mtbf-mttr/)
- The ICSA 2020 microservice paper uses the same relation to derive a CTMC rate from an availability target: MRR = AV / (MTBF (1 − AV)). [src] — [ICSA 2020 PDF](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~jcm567/files/ICSA2020.pdf)

**Tail latency amplification (Dean and Barroso, "The Tail at Scale", CACM Feb 2013, vol. 56 no. 2)**
- "If a user request must collect responses from 100 such servers in parallel, then 63% of user requests will take more than one second" (the "such servers" being ones with a one-in-100 chance of a >1 s response). "Even for services with only one in 10,000 requests experiencing more than one-second latencies at the single-server level, a service with 2,000 such servers will see almost one in five user requests taking more than one second". Figure 1 is titled "Probability of one-second service-level response time as the system scales and frequency of server-level high-latency outliers varies", with curves for 1 in 100, 1 in 1,000, 1 in 10,000 and marked values 0.63 and 0.18. [src] — [The Tail at Scale PDF (Barroso)](https://www.barroso.org/publications/TheTailAtScale.pdf); abstract page at [research.google](https://research.google/pubs/the-tail-at-scale/)
- Table 1 (measured, real Google fan-out service, from the root): one random leaf finishes at 1 ms / 5 ms / 10 ms (50th / 95th / 99th percentile); 95% of all leaf requests finish at 12 / 32 / 70 ms; 100% of all leaf requests finish at 40 / 87 / 140 ms. [src] — [The Tail at Scale PDF](https://www.barroso.org/publications/TheTailAtScale.pdf)
- Hedged requests: "sending a hedging request after a 10ms delay reduces the 99.9th-percentile latency for retrieving all 1,000 values from 1,800ms to 74ms while sending just 2% more requests" (1,000 keys in a BigTable table across 100 servers). Tied requests: client sends to two servers "each tagged with the identity of the other"; when one begins execution it sends a cancellation to its counterpart; "There is a brief window of one average network message delay where both servers may start executing the request". [src] — [The Tail at Scale PDF](https://www.barroso.org/publications/TheTailAtScale.pdf)
- Kleppmann, *Designing Data-Intensive Applications*, ch. 1: "Even if only a small percentage of backend calls are slow, the chance of getting a slow call increases with an increase in backend calls, so a higher proportion of end-user requests end up being slow. This is known as tail latency amplification"; "it takes just one slow call to make the entire end-user request slow"; recommends percentiles (p95, p99, p999) over averages. [sum, via reader notes] — [danlebrero book notes](https://danlebrero.com/2021/09/01/designing-data-intensive-applications-summary/); [mkassm chapter notes](https://github.com/mkassm/designing-data-intensive-applications-notes/tree/main/Chapters/Chapter%201%20-%20Reliable%2C%20Scalable%2C%20and%20Maintainable%20Applications)

### Inferences
- The fan-out numbers are exactly 1 − (1 − p)^n under independence: 1 − 0.99^100 = 0.634 and 1 − (1 − 10^-4)^2000 = 0.181, matching the 0.63 and 0.18 marked on Figure 1; the paper does not print the formula, so the formula is an inference, the numbers are read.
- Every SRE-workbook quantity is a ratio of counts over a finite window (bad events / total events, burn rate = observed ratio / budgeted ratio, budget consumed = burn rate × window / period). With a fixed denominator (e.g. parts per million) all of it is natural-number arithmetic: "14.4 × 1 h / 30 d = 2%" is 144 × 3600 / (10 × 2 592 000) in integers. This is directly stateable in Lean 4 core (see Key question 7).
- Series availability as a product, parallel as a product of complements, and fan-out tail probability as 1 − ∏(1 − p_i) are all the same algebraic fact (independence of components) applied to different events (up, down, slow); the independence assumption is what a model must make explicit and is usually false for correlated failures.
- Percentiles do not compose arithmetically: the p99 of a fan-out call is not a function of the children's p99s alone (it depends on the whole distribution and on n); the Tail at Scale table shows the 99th percentile growing from 10 ms (one leaf) to 140 ms (all leaves). Any formal treatment must carry distributions, not percentiles.

### Gaps
- The SRE Workbook page was read via a fetch summary; the table values and the formula match memory of the chapter but the chapter's wording around "burn rate" was not quoted verbatim.
- The MTBF/MTTR series/parallel formulas come from vendor and blog sources (BMC, QNX), not from a reliability-engineering textbook; the parallel MTBF approximation MTBF²/(2·MTTR) in particular should be checked against a textbook (e.g. Trivedi) before use.
- Kleppmann's exact wording was read only through third-party notes, not the book.

## Key question 3: Performance algebras (PEPA, stochastic process algebras, queueing models for microservices) and their mechanization

### Takeaway
PEPA gives a compositional syntax (prefix, choice, cooperation, hiding) whose operational semantics generates a CTMC from which throughput, utilisation and response time are read off via rewards, and it is supported by PRISM and Mobius; no formalization of PEPA or of any stochastic process algebra in a proof assistant was found.

### Cited Findings
- PEPA "is an expressive formal language for modelling distributed systems"; it enables "construction and solution of descriptive models" to "determine whether a candidate design meets both the behavioural and the temporal requirements demanded of it." Its three stated characteristics: compositionality ("the ability to model a system as the interaction of subsystems"), formality, abstraction. A PEPA model generates "a corresponding continuous time Markov chain (CTMC) by elaborating the model against the structured operational semantics of the PEPA language", and performance measures are derived "from this distribution via a reward structure defined over the CTMC". Tools: the PEPA Eclipse Plug-in; support in the Mobius Modeling Framework, the PRISM probabilistic model checker, and CADP. [sum, quotations relayed by the fetch tool] — [PEPA: about](http://www.dcs.ed.ac.uk/pepa/about/)
- Wikipedia summary: PEPA is "a stochastic process algebra designed for modeling computer and communication systems introduced by Jane Hillston in the 1990s, extending classical process algebras by introducing probabilistic branching and timing of transitions." [sum] — [Wikipedia: PEPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PEPA)
- Fluid (ODE) approximation of PEPA: "Fundamental Results on Fluid Approximations of Stochastic Process Algebra Models" exists as an arXiv paper (title only). [sum] — [arXiv 1008.4754](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1008.4754)
- A targeted search for a PEPA formalization in Isabelle, Coq or Agda returned no such work; the only nearby hits were mechanizations of non-stochastic process algebras for network protocols ("Mechanizing a Process Algebra for Network Protocols", [arXiv 1512.07304](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.07304)) and interaction-tree simulations in Isabelle ([arXiv 2105.05133](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.05133)). [sum]
- The ICSA 2020 paper's related work notes that "the automated translation of failure-retry blocks in the Palladio Component Model [21] offers a similar solution to the Retry pattern", i.e. Palladio (a component-based performance modelling framework) is the other established route to microservice performance models. [src] — [ICSA 2020 PDF](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~jcm567/files/ICSA2020.pdf)
- Related mechanized performance-adjacent work found by search (titles only): "Verifying Asymptotic Time Complexity of Imperative Programs in Isabelle" (Zhan and Haslbeck, IJCAR 2018) [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-94205-6_35); "Formalizing O Notation in Isabelle/HOL" (Avigad and Donnelly) [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225175316_Formalizing_O_Notation_in_IsabelleHOL). [sum]

### Inferences
- PEPA's cooperation operator is the algebraic form of the PRISM-module composition used in the microservice paper: components synchronise on shared action types and the semantics is the product CTMC. For a cell model the natural translation is one PEPA component per cell, with event names as action types and reaction rates as the exponential rates.
- Because PEPA's meaning is the CTMC, a mechanization would reduce to the Isabelle Markov_Models machinery plus a structural-operational-semantics layer; the absence of any such mechanization suggests it is a research gap rather than a known-hard problem.
- Classic queueing results (Little's law L = λW, M/M/1 response time 1/(μ − λ)) are the usual closed forms for throughput and latency under load [mem]; they are closed-form only under exponential assumptions, which is the same limitation the ICSA 2020 authors state ("Models assume exponential inter-arrival and service time distributions" [sum]).

### Gaps
- The PEPA "about" page fetched does not list the syntax or the specific measures; the syntax (prefix `(α, r).P`, choice `P + Q`, cooperation `P ⋈_L Q`, hiding `P/L`) is [mem] only.
- No source was found for queueing-network models applied specifically to microservices beyond the ICSA 2020 reference to Palladio and a systematic mapping study title ([MDPI 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/11/17/7856), not read); layered queueing networks (Woodside/Franks) are [mem].
- No mechanization of PEPA, of stochastic process algebras generally, or of queueing theory (Little's law etc.) in any proof assistant was found; absence of search hits is weak evidence.

## Key question 4: Resource and cost semantics (RAML/AARA, time credits, cost-as-a-type, WCET) and Lean 4 work on cost bounds

### Takeaway
Cost is formalized either externally (a type system with potential, as in RAML, that infers polynomial bounds by LP) or internally (time credits `$n` in separation logic, consumed one per `tick`/`pay()`, with soundness "a program granted n credits runs at most n steps"); the one Lean 4 cost formalization found (lean4-camus, 2026) uses a cost monad and Mathlib, and Lean 4 core offers only `Nat`/`Int` arithmetic with `omega` as the decision procedure.

### Cited Findings

**RAML / AARA**
- RaML "automatically and statically computes resource-use bounds for OCaml programs"; it derives "multivariate resource polynomials that are functions of different size parameters", both upper bounds on worst-case and lower bounds on best-case use; resource metrics are "the number of evaluation steps in a high-level abstract machine, allocated heap cells, and user-defined _ticks_ executed"; inference "is reduced to a linear optimization problem that is passed to an off-the-shelf LP solver"; it "supports higher-order, polymorphic programs with side effects and user-defined inductive types"; since "symbolic bound inference is an undecidable problem", RaML "cannot automatically derive a bound for every program". [sum, quotations relayed] — [RaML: about](https://www.raml.co/about/)
- Hoffmann, Das, Weng, "Towards Automatic Resource Bound Analysis for OCaml" (POPL 2017): "the analysis system is based on a novel multivariate automatic amortized resource analysis (AARA)"; it "can derive bounds for time, memory allocations and energy usage"; "the first amortized analysis that automatically derives polynomial bounds for higher-order functions and polynomial bounds that depend on user-defined inductive types." [sum] — [arXiv 1611.00692](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.00692); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3009837.3009842)
- Related AARA work surfaced (titles only): "Exponential Automatic Amortized Resource Analysis" [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-45231-5_19); "Automatic Linear Resource Bound Analysis for Rust via Prophecy Potentials" [arXiv 2502.19810](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.19810); "Resource-Bounded Type Theory: Compositional Cost Analysis via Graded Modalities" [arXiv 2512.06952](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06952) (Dec 2025, the most recent "cost as a type" entry found). [sum]

**Time credits (Coq/CFML, Iris)**
- Guéneau, Charguéraud, Pottier, "A Fistful of Dollars: Formalizing Asymptotic Complexity Claims via Deductive Program Verification" (ESOP 2018): "Separation Logic with Time Credits, a simple extension of Separation Logic where the assertion $1 represents a permission to perform one step of computation, and is consumed when exercised. The assertion $n is a separating conjunction of n such time credits." It is "implemented in ... CFML, which is embedded in the Coq proof assistant." The code is instrumented so that "a pay() instruction has been inserted at the beginning of every function body and loop body. The proof of the program is carried out under the axiom {$1} (pay()) {λ_.⊤}"; e.g. "Executing 'length l' involves executing pay() exactly |l| + 1 times." [src] — [ESOP 2018 PDF](https://armael.deuxfleurs.page/publis/gueneau-chargueraud-pottier-coq-bigO.pdf)
- Their motivating example: the earlier Union-Find verification's precondition "indicates that calling find requires and consumes $(2α(n) + 4)"; exposing that "is undesirable: if a tiny modification of the Union-Find module changes this cost to 2α(n) + 5, then all direct and indirect clients of the Union-Find module must be updated". [src] — [ESOP 2018 PDF](https://armael.deuxfleurs.page/publis/gueneau-chargueraud-pottier-coq-bigO.pdf)
- O notation formalized as "a binary domination relation between functions of type A → Z, where the type A is chosen by the user. Functions of several variables are covered by instantiating A with a product type ... the type A must be equipped with a filter". Specification style: "∃cost : Z → Z. cost ⪯_Z λn.n ∧ ∀(A : Type)(l : list A). {$cost(|l|)} (length l) {λy. [y = |l|]}". The `specO` record: "Record specO (A : filterType) (le : A → A → Prop) (bound : A → Z) (P : (A → Z) → Prop) := { cost : A → Z; cost_spec : P cost; cost_dominated : dominated A cost bound; cost_nonneg : ∀x, 0 ≤ cost x; cost_monotonic : monotonic le Z.le cost }". Concrete statement: "Theorem length_spec : specO Z_filterType Z.le (fun n ⇒ n) (fun cost ⇒ ∀A (l:list A), triple (length l) PRE ($ (cost |l|)) POST (fun y ⇒ [ y = |l| ]))". Examples include binary search and Bellman-Ford ("The example of the Bellman-Ford algorithm (§6) illustrates this"). Limitation acknowledged: "a loop that counts up from 0 to 2^60 has complexity O(1), even though it typically won't terminate in a lifetime." [src] — [ESOP 2018 PDF](https://armael.deuxfleurs.page/publis/gueneau-chargueraud-pottier-coq-bigO.pdf)
- Mével, Jourdan, Pottier, "Time Credits and Time Receipts in Iris" (2019): "a machine-checked extension of the program logic Iris with time credits and time receipts, two dual means of reasoning about time. Whereas time credits are used to establish an upper bound on a program's execution time, time receipts can be used to establish a lower bound. ### Inferences
- Both time credits and AARA are "cost as a Nat (or Int) resource consumed per step"; the proof-assistant versions are the credit style, and the only inputs they need are a step-counting instrumentation (`tick`/`pay`) and arithmetic on the credit count. - lean4-camus demonstrates that a Lean 4 cost monad plus Mathlib can carry a paper-level complexity claim through; its Mathlib dependence appears to come from graph theory and asymptotics, not from the cost monad itself.

## Key question 5: Probability in proof assistants (Mathlib PMF and measure theory, Isabelle, Coq ALEA/Polaris/FCF, SampCert)

### Takeaway
Mathlib's `PMF α` is `{f : α → ℝ≥0∞ // HasSum f 1}` with a monad and a `toMeasure` bridge into full measure theory; Isabelle has the most complete mechanized Markov-chain and pCTL library; Coq has ALEA (monadic measures) and Polaris (concurrent probabilistic separation logic); and SampCert is the worked Lean 4 example, 12,000+ lines of proof of differential privacy for discrete Laplace and Gaussian samplers, deployed at AWS, and built heavily on Mathlib.

### Cited Findings

## Key question 6: Statistical verification and conformance (SMC, hypothesis tests on sampled runs, the SLO as per-run predicate plus population measure)

### Takeaway
Statistical model checking treats each run as a Bernoulli trial for a Boolean per-run predicate and answers either a hypothesis test (Wald's SPRT with error strengths α, β and an indifference region) or an estimate with a Chernoff-Hoeffding sample size; an SLO such as "99% within an hour over 30 days" has exactly that shape, with the window's actual runs as the population and the sample-confidence bound only needed when the window is sampled rather than fully observed.

### Cited Findings
- UPPAAL-SMC (Legay et al., "Statistical Model Checking for Priced Timed Automata", arXiv 1207.1272): three questions: "1. Hypothesis Testing: Is the probability P_M(◇_{x≤C} φ) for a given NPTA M greater or equal to a certain threshold p ∈ [0,1]? 2. Probability evaluation: What is the probability ...? 3. Probability comparison". "First, each run of the system is encoded as a Bernoulli random variable that is true if the run satisfies the property and false otherwise". [src] — [arXiv 1207.1272 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.1272)
- Hypothesis testing: "we test the hypothesis H0 : p ≥ p0 and H1 : p ≤ p1 with p0 = θ + δ0 and p1 = θ − δ1. The interval p0 − p1 defines an indifference region ... The parameter α is the probability of accepting H0 when H1 holds (false positives) and the parameter β is the probability of accepting H1 when H0 holds (false negatives). The above test can be solved by using Wald's sequential hypothesis testing. ... With probability 1, the value of the proportion will eventually cross log(β/(1 − α)) or log((1 − β)/α) and one of the two hypothesis will be selected." Query: `Pr[bound](φ) >= p0`, where the bound is "1) implicitly by time by specifying <=M ..., 2) explicitly by cost with x<=M where x is a specific clock, or 3) by number of discrete steps with #<=M"; φ is `<> q` or `[] q`. [src] — [arXiv 1207.1272 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.1272)
- Probability estimation: "computes the number of runs needed in order to produce an approximation interval [p − ε, p + ε] for p = Pr(ψ) with a confidence 1 − α. The values of ε and α are chosen by the user and the number of runs relies on the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound." Query `Pr[bound](φ)`. Expected values: `E[bound; N](min:expr)` / `E[bound; N](max:expr)` where N is the number of runs; "For this property, no confidence is given (yet)." Example: `Pr[<=300](<> Gate.len < 3 and t > 20)` returned "The probability is in [0.102,0.123]". [src] — [arXiv 1207.1272 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.1272)
- Chernoff-Hoeffding sample-size relation as given in SMC tutorials: "the parameter δ is related to the number of simulations N by δ = 2e^{−2Nε²}", giving "P(|p̂ − p| ≥ ε) ≤ δ". [sum] — [Statistical Model Checking (Springer chapter)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-91908-9_23); [Statistical Model Checking for Biological Applications](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1405.2705)
- Plasma-related and other SMC tools surfaced (titles only): "Distributed Parametric and Statistical Model Checking" [arXiv 1111.0370](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.0370); "Statistical Model Checking Meets Property-Based Testing" [ICST 2017](https://truconf.sai.tugraz.at/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2017/02/icst2017.pdf); "Learning and statistical model checking of system response times" [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330115750_Learning_and_statistical_model_checking_of_system_response_times); "Checking Response-Time Properties of Web-Service Applications Under Stochastic User Profiles" [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-67549-7_18). [sum]
- SRE practice side of the same object: the SLI used in the workbook is an error ratio over a window, `job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate1h`, compared to `burn_rate * 0.001`. [sum] — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)

### Inferences
- Solving δ = 2e^{−2Nε²} for N gives N ≥ ln(2/δ) / (2ε²). For an SLO target of 0.99 with a desired half-width ε = 0.005 and δ = 0.05, N ≥ ln(40)/(2 × 2.5e−5) ≈ 73,778 runs; this is why SMC is an estimation tool for simulators and why production SLOs are computed on the full population of observed requests rather than sampled.
- Decomposition of "99% within an hour over a 30-day window": (i) a per-run Boolean predicate `good(run) := completed(run) ∧ latency(run) ≤ 1 h`, which is a trace predicate; (ii) a population: the multiset of runs that started in the window; (iii) a measure: the ratio |{good}| / |population| ≥ 0.99. In SMC vocabulary (i) is φ, (ii)+(iii) is `Pr[<=window](φ) >= 0.99` evaluated under a model that generates runs; in SRE vocabulary (ii)+(iii) is the SLI over the window. The model-checking version (PRISM `P>=0.99 [F<=3600 "done"]`) quantifies over the model's distribution of runs; the SRE version quantifies over actual runs; the SMC version bridges them with a confidence bound.
- The qualitative SPRT form `Pr[...](φ) >= p0` with an indifference region is the exact statistical analogue of a burn-rate alert: both decide "is the error ratio above threshold" from a stream of Bernoulli outcomes with controlled false-positive/false-negative rates, and the SRE multiwindow rule is a heuristic substitute for the sequential test.

### Gaps
- The exact Chernoff-Hoeffding formula was read in a search summary of a tutorial chapter, not in the UPPAAL-SMC paper, which only names the bound.
- No source was found that explicitly connects SRE error budgets to statistical model checking; the connection above is the researcher's inference.
- Plasma Lab documentation was not fetched.

## Key question 7 (synthesis for the reader): what quantitative claims are reachable in Lean 4 core (Nat, Int) versus with Mathlib

### Takeaway
Everything the SRE Workbook does (counts, ratios with fixed denominators, burn rates, budget consumed, series/parallel availability of concrete numbers, cost-per-operation totals, explicit-constant complexity bounds) is statable and provable in Lean 4 core over `Nat`/`Int`, with `omega` discharging the linear parts and `decide`/`norm_num`-free evaluation the concrete ones; anything that quantifies over a distribution of future runs (probability a run completes, expected latency, percentiles of a distribution, independence, steady-state availability of a CTMC, big-O with filters, Hoeffding confidence) needs Mathlib's `ℝ≥0∞`, `PMF`, measure theory and analysis.

### Cited Findings
- Lean core arithmetic automation: `omega` is in Lean core since 4.7 and decides linear arithmetic over `Nat` and `Int` with constant multiplication, `/` and `%` by literals, and case splits on natural subtraction; nonlinear subterms are atoms. [sum] — [Lean 4.8.0 release post](https://lean-lang.org/blog/2024-6-1-lean-480/); [Lean.Elab.Tactic.Omega docs](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Lean/Elab/Tactic/Omega.html)
- Mathlib probability lives on `ℝ≥0∞` (`PMF α = {f : α → ℝ≥0∞ // HasSum f 1}`) and the measure-theory bridge `PMF.toMeasure`; the file imports `MeasureTheory.Measure.Dirac.Basic`. [sum] — [Mathlib PMF docs](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Probability/ProbabilityMassFunction/Basic.html)
- SampCert, the one large Lean 4 quantitative-guarantee project found, "makes heavy use of Mathlib" and uses results such as the Poisson summation formula. [sum] — [GitHub leanprover/SampCert](https://github.com/leanprover/SampCert); abstract [arXiv 2412.01671](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01671)
- lean4-camus, the one Lean 4 cost-bound project found, uses a cost monad and depends on Mathlib. [sum] — [GitHub vltanh/lean4-camus](https://github.com/vltanh/lean4-camus)
- Time-credit style cost reasoning needs only "$n is a separating conjunction of n such time credits" and the rule {$1} pay() {⊤}; the ESOP 2018 specifications are over `Z`-valued cost functions. [src] — [ESOP 2018 PDF](https://armael.deuxfleurs.page/publis/gueneau-chargueraud-pottier-coq-bigO.pdf)
- All SRE quantities are ratios over a window ("burn rate × alerting window size / period"; threshold `ratio_rate1h > 14.4 * 0.001`). [sum] — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)

### Inferences (the core-versus-Mathlib map; each row is the researcher's judgement from the findings above, with the Lean-core details [mem])
- **Reliability as an observed fraction over a finite trace set.** `good : Run → Bool`, `runs : List Run`; claim `(runs.filter good).length * 100 ≥ 99 * runs.length`. Core only. Decidable for concrete traces (`decide`/`by native_decide` or `rfl`-style evaluation); for symbolic bounds (`∀ runs, invariant runs → ...`) linear in the lengths, `omega`.
- **Error budget, burn rate, budget consumed.** Represent the SLO error ratio as a pair `(num, den)` of `Nat` (e.g. 1 per 1000); burn rate as `errors * den` versus `num * total` cross-multiplied; budget consumed by a window as `burn * window_secs * 100 / period_secs` percent. Core only; `omega` handles the constant multiplications; the SRE table (14.4 × 3600 / 2 592 000 = 2%) is `decide`-able once 14.4 is written as 144/10.
- **Series availability of concrete components.** With availabilities as `Nat` per million, `A_series = A1 * A2 / 1_000_000` is computable, but the equality is nonlinear in symbolic variables; `omega` will not prove `a * b ≤ a` for variables, but core lemmas (`Nat.mul_le_mul_left`, `Nat.div_le_self`) do, and monotonicity bounds ("series availability ≤ each component") are short core proofs. Parallel (`1 − (1 − a)(1 − b)`) is the same with complements; care with `Nat` subtraction truncation.
- **Tail probability under fan-out.** `1 − (1 − p)^n` with `p` rational and `n : Nat` is computable in core with `Nat.pow` on scaled integers for concrete `p, n` (e.g. `99^100` fits in `Nat`), but the inequality "63% ≥ ..." for symbolic `n` needs Bernoulli-inequality style lemmas that are easy over `Nat` only for the linear lower bound `(1 − p)^n ≥ 1 − n p`; the exact asymptotics need reals (Mathlib).
- **Cost per operation and total cost of a trace.** A `cost : Event → Nat` and `List.sum` (or `foldl`) over the trace; bounds `total ≤ c * trace.length + d` are linear and `omega`-provable; quadratic bounds need explicit `Nat.mul_le_mul`. Core only. This is exactly the time-credit discipline (each reaction "pays" its credits) minus the separation logic; an amortized bound is a potential function `Φ : State → Nat` with `cost + Φ s' ≤ k + Φ s`, also core-expressible.
- **Explicit-constant complexity.** `∃ c n₀, ∀ n ≥ n₀, cost n ≤ c * f n` is core; hidden-constant big-O with filters (`Asymptotics.IsBigO`, `Filter.atTop`, product filters for multivariate as in the ESOP 2018 paper) is Mathlib [mem for the Mathlib names].
- **Probability a run completes, under a model that generates runs.** Needs a distribution over runs: Mathlib `PMF` (or at least `ℝ≥0∞`, which is `WithTop NNReal`, which needs `Real`). Core cannot state it except as exact rationals on a finite DTMC: a finite chain with rational transition weights `(num, den)` and k-step reachability as a finite sum of products is core-computable (Nat arithmetic on scaled weights, e.g. per 10^6), and a theorem "the k-step completion probability of this concrete chain is ≥ 0.99" is `decide`-able for small k; general lemmas (law of total probability, independence, monotonicity in k) are then hand-proved algebra, which is where Mathlib's `Finset.sum` lemmas and `PMF.bind` would save effort. Note Lean core has `Nat`/`Int` and `Float` (no proofs about Float); `Rat` is in Batteries not core [mem, check: it may have moved].
- **Expected latency, percentiles, throughput under load, steady-state availability of a CTMC.** All need reals (exponential rates, limits, linear-system solutions): Mathlib. Latency *distributions over a finite observed trace set* (e.g. "the 99th-percentile observed latency is ≤ 1 h", defined as "at most 1% of observed runs exceed 1 h") are back to the core-only counting case.
- **Confidence of a sample (Hoeffding).** Needs `exp` and reals: Mathlib (Hoeffding is believed present [mem]). Core can state the *decision rule* of a sequential test on counts, but not its error-probability guarantee.
- **Composition across cells.** For observed-trace claims composition is set-theoretic (the trace of the composed system restricts to each cell's trace) and core; for distributional claims independence must be assumed or proved, and the product distribution needs `PMF.bind`/`Measure.prod` (Mathlib).

### Gaps
- Whether `Rat` now lives in Lean core or Batteries, and whether Mathlib has Hoeffding's inequality under that name, were not verified this session.
- No published Lean 4 project was found that does SRE-style SLO arithmetic or series/parallel availability over `Nat`; the core-only route above is the researcher's construction, not a cited precedent.
- No source was found that compares "observed-fraction over a finite window" with "probability under a model" as alternative formal readings of an SLO; the distinction is the researcher's.
